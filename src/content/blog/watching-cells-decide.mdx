I want to talk about a problem that has bothered me for a while, and it is a simple one to state. You take a piece of tissue, you sequence the cells in it, and you get back a table: a few thousand cells, each one measured exactly once. Once, because to read the genes inside a cell you have to break it open, and then it is dead. From that table you draw a map, the usual cloud of colored blobs, and you can read off where each cell sits. These maps are good. Most of what we know about which cells live in a tissue, we got from them.

Here is what they cannot tell you. Take one cell, sitting at one spot on the map. Which way is it going? Is it a young cell on its way to becoming a neutrophil, or a cell that set out to become one and got stuck partway? Those two are the same dot, the same color, and you cannot tell them apart. In a healthy tissue, maybe you do not need to. But in leukemia you need to badly, because when we say the marrow has gone wrong, we mostly mean something about motion: cells that are supposed to be moving toward a mature fate are just sitting there. A photograph does not show motion.

So here is the question I want to ask. Can I take a cell I only caught once, dead, and still say which way it was heading? I think the answer is mostly yes. But to see why, you have to stop thinking about the cell as a dot on a map and start thinking about it as a dynamical system. Let me show you what I mean, starting as small as I can.

## Start with the simplest thing

Forget genes for a minute. I want the simplest picture that still has the idea in it.

<ToyField client:visible />

Every little arrow says which way a point moves next. Let go of a point anywhere and it follows the arrows. Now look at where they lead. There are two spots the arrows run into and stop. Drop a point near one of them and it slides in and stays; give it a small shove and it comes right back. A spot like that is called an attractor. And there is one spot in the middle that the arrows run away from. Balance a point exactly on it and the smallest push sends it off to one side or the other. That is a repeller. The whole plane divides in two by which attractor you end up in, and each half is a basin. The line down the middle, the fence between the two basins, is the separatrix. That is the whole vocabulary I need: attractors, a repeller, basins, a separatrix. Four words.

Now here is the claim I want to make. Two genes are enough to build exactly this picture, and when they do, the picture is a cell choosing its fate.

Take a blood cell that has not yet decided whether to become a white cell or a red one. Inside it are two genes, PU.1 and GATA1, and I picked this pair on purpose. It is one of the best-studied fate decisions there is: PU.1 runs the white-cell program, GATA1 runs the red-cell program, and the two proteins physically grab hold of each other and shut each other down, which people have measured directly in the lab <Citation n={16} />. So the wiring really is this simple: each gene turns itself on, and each gene turns the other off. That is all of it. And it matters that this is a real decision and not a toy I invented, because this exact pair is where the abstract switch I am about to draw was first pinned to actual genes and worked out as a dynamical system <Citation n={17} />. You can see, without any biology, why it is enough to force a choice. Suppose PU.1 gets a little ahead, for no reason in particular. Then it presses harder on GATA1, so GATA1 drops, and a smaller GATA1 presses back on PU.1 less, so PU.1 climbs higher, which pushes GATA1 down further. It runs away with itself. Let it go and it always ends up at one of two extremes: PU.1 up and GATA1 off, or the other way around. One is a white cell, the other is red. The one thing it will not do is sit in the middle with the two genes balanced. The middle is the place it runs away from.

Let me draw it the same way I drew the toy. Write down how fast each gene changes, put PU.1 along one axis and GATA1 up the other, and every state of the cell is a point on the plane. Here are the equations, in case you want them. With $x$ for PU.1 and $y$ for GATA1,

$$
\dot{x} = \alpha\,\frac{x^n}{\theta^n + x^n} + \beta\,\frac{\theta^n}{\theta^n + y^n} - k\,x,
\qquad
\dot{y} = \alpha\,\frac{y^n}{\theta^n + y^n} + \beta\,\frac{\theta^n}{\theta^n + x^n} - k\,y.
$$

The first term on each line is a gene turning itself on, the second is the other gene turning it off, the last is the protein wearing away. That is the whole model. Here is its picture.

<PhasePortrait client:visible />

It is the toy over again, with real genes in it. Two attractors, one in each corner, a white cell and a red one. The only difference is the middle. In the toy that was a plain repeller. Here it is a saddle, which just means the cell falls toward it from two directions and away from it along the other two. Same idea, one more dimension: a spot the cell cannot rest on. That spot is the progenitor. I want to be careful about it, because it is easy to get backwards. The undecided progenitor is not a valley the cell sits in. It is the ridge it rolls off.

And now the question is the same as it was for the toy. Start a cell somewhere, and which fate does it reach? Follow the arrows and you land in one corner or the other, so every point on the plane belongs to a fate. Color the plane by that fate, which is what the "show fate map" button does, and it splits into two basins with the separatrix between them. A cell a step to one side of that line becomes white; a step to the other, red. On the map I started with, those two cells could be neighbors, the same color, and you could not tell them apart. In the dynamics they were never going to the same place. The fate is in the flow, not in the position.

## The same thing as a landscape

There is an older picture that says this, and it is worth connecting to, because you have probably seen it. In 1957 Conrad Waddington drew a cell's fate as a ball rolling down a hilly landscape, picking a valley at each fork and never rolling back up <Citation n={1} />. Muscle down one valley, blood down another. He drew it by hand, as a picture, because he did not have the genes. It took a long time before anyone could show it was more than a picture: that a cell type really does behave like an attractor of the gene network, a state the dynamics fall into and hold <Citation n={2} />.

We can build Waddington's landscape now, out of the two genes. The trick is to squeeze them down to one number. Let $\Delta$ be PU.1 minus GATA1, the lead of one gene over the other: zero in the middle, large one way for one fate, large the other way for the other. Then you can write down a height, a potential, that the cell rolls down. The bottoms of the valleys are the attractors. The tops of the hills are the saddles.

<PotentialLandscape client:visible />

Now watch what the landscape does as I turn one knob. Start with the knob high and there is a single valley in the middle. A cell rolls to the bottom and sits there, and here the progenitor really is a resting place, a genuine valley, which is how a stem cell manages to stay a stem cell instead of falling apart into something else right away. That is the multipotent cell. Now turn the knob down. At a sharp value the single valley humps up in the middle and splits into two. The old resting spot is now a hilltop, and the cell cannot stay on it. It has to roll down one side. That splitting of one valley into two, at a threshold, is called a bifurcation, and it is exactly Waddington's fork in the road. And notice where the fork is. It is not in the cell. It is in the landscape, and it shows up when the signal crosses a value. Differentiation, in this picture, is a sequence of these splits, run in order.

I should be plain about one thing, because a physicist will ask. The two-gene system does not really roll down a fixed height in the strict sense; a true potential only exists in special cases. What I am drawing is an effective potential along $\Delta$. It gets the shape right and it is a standard move, but it is a reduction, not the actual energy of the cell.

## What a disease looks like here

So what is a disease, in this language? Here is one clean answer: the landscape is wrong. A valley that is supposed to be shallow and temporary is instead deep, and a cell that rolls in cannot climb out. Or a fork that should send a cell on to a mature fate is flattened, so cells pile up in front of it.

Leukemia looks a lot like that. In acute myeloid leukemia the cells wear the marks of early progenitors and mostly never mature; they stall in states they were supposed to pass through. When people sequenced a large amount of AML marrow, they found the malignant cells spread across the same hierarchy as healthy blood, but with the traffic sent to the wrong places, piling up early <Citation n={14} />. Huang and his colleagues made this argument years ago, and I think it is the right way to think about cancer <Citation n={3} />. The cell is not inventing some brand-new state out of nothing. It is running the normal landscape wrong: a basin worn too deep, a ridge worn too low. That changes the question you ask. Instead of only asking which genes are mutated, you ask what the mutation does to the shape of the landscape, and so to the flow. A mutation that barely touches the genes you would sort cells by can still be a catastrophe if it turns a resting spot into a trap. You would not catch that by comparing lists of genes between clusters. You would catch it by comparing the flow.

## Getting the flow out of real cells

Everything so far is a model. Two genes, a clean equation, a landscape I drew by hand. The real question, the one I started with, is whether you can get any of this out of actual cells, which, remember, you only ever caught once, dead.

Some of it you can. Start with just putting the cells in order. If becoming a blood cell takes a few days, and you catch thousands of cells at one instant, then you caught some early, some late, and everything in between. Sort them by how far along they look and you have rebuilt a rough timeline you never actually filmed. That is called pseudotime, and it works well enough that people use it all the time <Citation n={11} /><Citation n={10} />. Somebody went and compared the many ways of doing it and found they really do differ in how well they get the order and the branch points right, so it is not automatic, but it is real <Citation n={12} />. Still, pseudotime only gives you an axis. It does not tell you which way along the axis is forward. It lays down the road without painting an arrow on it.

For the arrow you need a cleverer idea, and one showed up around 2018 <Citation n={6} />. It uses an accident of the measurement. When a gene switches on, the first RNA the cell makes still has its introns in it, and the cell splices them out afterward. Ordinary sequencing counts both the unspliced RNA and the spliced RNA. So for each gene, in each cell, you can compare how much fresh unspliced message there is against how much finished spliced message. If a gene is turning on, there is too much unspliced. If it is turning off, too little. And that imbalance tells you which way the gene is heading, out of a single frozen snapshot.

Write it as a simple rate. If $u$ is unspliced message and $s$ is spliced,

$$
\dot{s} = \beta u - \gamma s,
$$

with $\beta$ the splicing rate and $\gamma$ the rate the message decays. The gap between the spliced count you actually see and the count the current unspliced would hold steady tells you $\dot{s}$: how fast, and which way, that gene is moving. Do it for every gene and you have a direction for the cell. And that direction is exactly the little arrow I drew by hand in the phase portrait, except now it is measured. scVelo made the estimate better by fitting each gene's own kinetics instead of assuming it sits at steady state <Citation n={7} />, and CellRank took the per-cell arrows and turned them into fate probabilities by letting cells wander along the flow <Citation n={8} />.

None of this is free, and I want to say where it breaks, because the people who built it did. The method assumes the genes have settled into a steady balance of splicing and decay somewhere in the data, and that the balance is about the same across the cells you looked at. Where nothing is changing, in resting blood instead of blood being made, there is no flow to find, and the method will still hand you arrows, and they will mean nothing <Citation n={9} />. It is an estimate, tuned for a system that is actually going somewhere. The further you get from that, the less you should believe it.

## Blood, where the flow is real

Blood is close to the best case for all of this, which is why it keeps turning up in these papers. One stem cell in the marrow gives rise, through a graded line of progenitors, to every kind of mature blood cell: red cells, platelets, the white cells. Early single-cell work already found more kinds of progenitor than the old tree allowed, each one already leaning toward a fate before it had formally committed <Citation n={4} />. Look inside the stem compartment and the sharp categories mostly melt away; commitment looks like a slow drift across a smooth landscape, with no clean instant where the cell decided <Citation n={15} />. Which is, almost exactly, the landscape we built by hand: one broad shallow valley at the top, forks partway down, separate valleys at the bottom.

One test first. Weinreb and his colleagues tagged cells with heritable barcodes and actually watched which fate the descendants took, instead of guessing it from the snapshot. What they found is that a cell's state predicts its fate only partway, and much better for cells already near a fate than for the young multipotent ones <Citation n={5} />. So position is a real clue. It is not a promise. Which is the whole reason you want the flow and not just the position.

<RealDataFigure client:visible citeN={13} />

These are real cells: 5,780 human bone marrow cells, sorted for CD34, a marker that picks out stem and early progenitor cells, which is exactly the population where the snapshot tells you the least. Setty and colleagues sequenced and ordered them <Citation n={13} />, and their data has become a standard thing to test velocity on. I ran it through the pipeline I described and drew the measured flow on top of the map.

Look at what the marks point to. The clusters the arrows pour into are the attractors from the toy, only now they are real mature blood cells. The colored regions feeding each one are the basins. And the little arrows are the flow, the same arrows I drew by hand for two genes, here measured for thousands of real cells at once. The position was always sitting there on the map, and now, finally, you can see which way each cell is going.

Two things to notice. First, most of the arrows point the way the known hierarchy says they should. That is the test the method has to pass before you trust it anywhere harder, and here it passes. Second, the weakest arrows are not where you would guess. They are not on the stem cells, whose arrows are among the longest and surest on the plot. They are on the thinnest branch, the one with the fewest cells, because velocity is worked out from each cell's neighbors, and a thin branch gives the method almost nothing to go on. The method tells you, if you look, exactly where its own signal runs out.

## Which way, not where

I am not against snapshots. They are most of what we have, and pseudotime and clustering and marker genes, all of it built from one frozen instant, taught us most of what we know about blood. My point is narrower. A snapshot answers where a cell is. A great many of the questions that actually matter are about which way it is going, and how fast, and those are not the same question.

And once you start asking which way, blood and cancer are only the first stop. An embryo turning into an animal is nothing but decisions like this, run over and over. So is a wound closing. So is an immune cell being switched on, or a resting fibroblast being pushed backward into a stem cell in a dish. Every one of these is a system moving through time, and for every one of them, knowing the direction beats knowing the position. The framework does not care that it started with blood. It cares that something is changing.

And the good part is that people are now getting the time back from both ends. On the bench, you can make cells carry their own history: CRISPR "recorders" that scar a cell's DNA a little more each time it divides, so its descendants spell out the lineage tree of a whole embryo <Citation n={20} />, and metabolic labels that tag only the RNA a cell made in the last hour, so you measure real elapsed time instead of guessing it <Citation n={19} />. On the computer, you can go past one arrow per cell and learn the whole vector field, and the landscape under it, and then use it to predict where a cell will go next <Citation n={21} />; or you can take snapshots from several days and sew them into a single flow with optimal transport <Citation n={18} />. RNA velocity and CellRank, from before, are early members of this family, not the end of it.

Here is what I want, in the end. To write down, for a real piece of tissue, both the landscape and the flow running on it. To say not just what a cell is, but what it is about to do. And to catch the moment a healthy decision starts bending toward a bad one, before the cell has finished making it. We are not there. But you can see the shape of it from here, and by golly it is a good problem, because it does not look easy.

You cannot tell, from one photograph, whether a thing is moving or only looks like it is. You have to catch it twice. We are finally learning how.
